# Financial Crime Case studies

## Case 1: Structuring Activity

**Scenario:**  
A customer deposits $9,800 cash multiple times per week.

**Analysis:**
- Repeated deposits just below the $10,000 threshold  
- Pattern suggests intentional structuring  
- Activity inconsistent with normal customer behavior  

**Conclusion:**  
This activity may indicate structuring to avoid reporting requirements under the Bank Secrecy Act.

---

## Case 2: Dormant Account Activity

**Scenario:**  
An account inactive for one year suddenly receives a $50,000 transfer.

**Analysis:**
- Sudden activity after long dormancy  
- Large transaction amount  
- Unusual compared to past behavior  

**Conclusion:**  
This activity may require further investigation due to unusual account behavior.

### Executive Summary

Artificial Intelligence (AI) is transforming the financial crime landscape. Financial institutions increasingly use AI to identify suspicious activity, detect fraud patterns, and strengthen transaction monitoring capabilities. At the same time, criminals are leveraging AI to create more sophisticated schemes, including synthetic identities, deepfake impersonation, and automated fraud attacks. This case explores both the opportunities and challenges AI presents for AML professionals.

### Financial Crime Risks Mentioned

| Risk Type | Description |
|------------|-------------|
| Synthetic Identity Fraud | Creation of fake identities using real and fabricated information |
| Deepfake Fraud | AI-generated voice or video impersonation |
| Account Takeover | Unauthorized access to customer accounts |
| Transaction Fraud | Suspicious movement of funds designed to avoid detection |
| Money Laundering | Concealing illicit proceeds through financial systems |

### AML Analyst Perspective

If I were reviewing these activities as an AML Analyst, I would focus on identifying:

- Unusual transaction frequency
- Sudden changes in customer behavior
- Rapid movement of funds
- Multiple accounts connected to one individual
- Transactions inconsistent with customer profile
- Transactions involving high-risk jurisdictions or unusual cross-border activity

The goal would be to determine whether the activity represents normal customer behavior or potentially suspicious activity requiring escalation.

### How AI Can Help AML Teams

| Traditional Approach | AI-Assisted Approach |
|---------------------|----------------------|
| Manual review | Automated anomaly detection |
| Rule-based alerts | Behavioral pattern analysis |
| Periodic monitoring | Real-time monitoring |
| Basic customer screening | Dynamic risk scoring |
| Spreadsheet tracking | Predictive analytics |

### Limitations and Risks of AI

While AI offers significant benefits, it also introduces challenges:

- False positive alerts
- Data quality issues
- Model bias
- Limited explainability
- Regulatory concerns
- Overreliance on automation
- Potential misuse by criminals

Financial institutions must balance technology with human oversight and risk-based judgment.

### Key Takeaways

- AI is improving fraud detection efficiency.
- Criminals are increasingly using AI-enabled techniques.
- Human judgment remains critical in AML investigations.
- Financial institutions must balance automation with oversight.
- AML professionals need both analytical and technology awareness skills.

### Personal Reflection

This case helped me understand how AI is changing financial crime detection and compliance operations. With 14 years of banking operations experience involving transaction review, exception handling, documentation controls, and fraud indicator awareness, I recognize that technology can improve efficiency but cannot completely replace human judgment.

My key takeaway is that future AML professionals will need both investigative thinking and an understanding of emerging technologies. AI should be viewed as a tool that supports analysts rather than replacing their decision-making responsibilities.

## Case 4: Sanctions Evasion Risk Through Cryptoasset Exchanges (2026)

## Disclaimer

This case analysis is based on publicly available information and is intended for educational and professional development purposes only. It does not represent legal, regulatory, sanctions, or compliance advice.

---

## Source

Original Source Reviewed: OFAC sanctions action involving Nobitex and Iranian cryptoasset exchanges

Topic Area: Sanctions evasion, cryptoasset monitoring, stablecoin activity, wallet-risk exposure, and AML transaction monitoring

---

## Executive Summary

This case analysis examines how sanctioned or high-risk actors may attempt to use domestic cryptoasset exchanges, stablecoins, self-hosted wallets, intermediary swaps, and cross-chain bridges to move value outside traditional banking channels.

What stood out to me is that sanctions risk may not always appear through a directly named customer or counterparty. In some situations, the risk may only become visible through transaction patterns, wallet exposure, or connections to higher-risk jurisdictions.

From an AML perspective, this case highlights the importance of looking beyond name-based screening and reviewing transaction behavior, customer profile consistency, counterparty exposure, and risk-based red flags.

---

## Scenario Overview

A financial institution provides banking services to a cryptoasset business. The cryptoasset business receives funds from self-hosted wallets that appear to be connected, within one or two transaction hops, to a domestic cryptoasset exchange operating in a sanctioned or high-risk jurisdiction.

The funds arrive as stablecoins, are converted quickly into another cryptoasset, and then moved through a cross-chain bridge before settlement.

No direct customer name appears on a sanctions list. However, the transaction pattern raises concerns because the activity may indicate indirect exposure to a sanctioned exchange, sanctions evasion risk, or an attempt to obscure the source and movement of funds.

---

## Financial Crime Risks Identified

| Risk Area                       | Why It Matters                                                                                |
| ------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Sanctions Evasion               | Sanctioned actors may move value indirectly through wallets, exchanges, or intermediaries.    |
| Stablecoin Use                  | Stablecoins allow value to remain dollar-denominated outside traditional banking channels.    |
| Wallet Cluster Exposure         | Risk may appear through wallet connections rather than customer names.                        |
| Cross-Chain Movement            | Moving funds across blockchains can make activity more difficult to trace.                    |
| High-Risk Jurisdiction Exposure | Activity connected to sanctioned or high-risk jurisdictions increases AML and sanctions risk. |
| Weak Name-Based Screening       | Name screening alone may not identify blockchain-related exposure.                            |

---

## Red Flags Identified

Potential red flags in this scenario include:

* Funds received from wallet clusters connected to a sanctioned or high-risk exchange
* Rapid conversion from stablecoins into other cryptoassets
* Cross-chain bridging shortly after receipt of funds
* Activity involving a high-risk or sanctioned jurisdiction without a clear business purpose
* Transaction behavior inconsistent with the customer profile
* Repeated use of self-hosted wallets with limited transparency
* Movement of funds through multiple intermediary wallets
* Lack of a clear economic purpose for rapid fund movement
* Concentrated exposure to high-risk counterparties
* Screening results that appear clean by name but show elevated blockchain-related risk

---

## Investigation Questions

If reviewing this case, I would consider the following questions:

1. Is the customer expected to receive funds from self-hosted wallets?
2. Does the customer have documented exposure to cryptoasset counterparties in high-risk jurisdictions?
3. Are the wallet connections direct, one-hop, or two-hop exposure?
4. Is there a pattern of rapid stablecoin conversion after funds are received?
5. Is cross-chain bridging common for this customer’s normal activity?
6. Are transaction amounts and frequency consistent with the customer profile?
7. Has the customer provided a reasonable explanation for the activity?
8. Are similar wallet clusters appearing across multiple transactions?
9. Does the activity involve any known sanctions or illicit-finance typologies?
10. Should the activity be escalated for enhanced review?

---

## AML Analyst Perspective

From my perspective, this case highlights the importance of reviewing the full context rather than focusing on a single red flag.

One unusual transaction does not automatically indicate suspicious activity, but the combination of wallet exposure, rapid movement of funds, cross-chain activity, and jurisdictional risk would likely warrant additional review.

Based on my banking operations experience, I would compare the activity against the customer profile, review available supporting information, evaluate whether the pattern is repeated, and determine whether escalation is appropriate under the institution’s procedures.

Additional investigation, customer information, and institutional procedures would be necessary before reaching that conclusion.

Factors that may warrant additional review include:

* Exposure to sanctioned or high-risk wallet clusters
* Repeated movement through self-hosted wallets with limited transparency
* Rapid conversion and movement of funds without a clear business purpose
* Activity inconsistent with the customer profile
* Attempts to obscure the source, ownership, or destination of funds
* Potential connections to sanctioned or illicit actors

---

## Operational Impact

This type of activity may affect several AML and sanctions compliance functions.

### Customer Due Diligence (CDD)

Financial institutions may need to better understand whether crypto-related activity is expected for the customer and whether the customer has exposure to high-risk jurisdictions or counterparties.

### Transaction Monitoring

Monitoring programs may need to consider wallet behavior, transaction velocity, stablecoin activity, and cross-chain movement in addition to traditional payment patterns.

### Sanctions Screening

Sanctions risk may not appear only through customer names. Institutions may need to evaluate wallet-level exposure and indirect blockchain connections.

### Investigation Procedures

Investigators may require clear procedures for reviewing wallet exposure, blockchain analytics alerts, customer explanations, and escalation decisions.

### Governance and Documentation

Institutions should maintain clear documentation supporting risk assessments, escalation decisions, and monitoring approaches.

---

## Key Takeaway

This case demonstrates that sanctions and AML risk can appear indirectly through transaction behavior, wallet exposure, and movement of value across cryptoasset networks.

For AML analysts, the key lesson is to combine transaction monitoring, customer due diligence, sanctions awareness, and risk-based judgment. Name-based screening remains important, but understanding the broader context of customer activity is equally critical.

## Case 5: Crypto Mixing Services and Transaction Monitoring Risk (2026)

## Disclaimer

This case analysis is based on publicly available information and is intended for educational and professional development purposes only. It does not represent legal, regulatory, compliance, or investigative advice.

---

## Source

Primary Source:

"Two Charged in Connection With Cryptocurrency Money Laundering Service That Allegedly Laundered Over $389 Million in Unlawful Transactions"

Published: June 11, 2026

Supporting Sources Reviewed:

* Europol
* Chainalysis
* FinCrime Intelligence Weekly Issue 3

---

## Executive Summary

This case examines the risks associated with cryptocurrency mixing services and the challenges they create for AML transaction monitoring.

What stood out to me is that the service marketed itself as a way to make cryptocurrency transactions untraceable. However, according to investigators, the activity was ultimately traced through blockchain analysis and exchange records.

Another observation from this case is that financial crime risk may not always be visible through customer names alone. In some situations, transaction behavior, wallet exposure, and connections between accounts may provide stronger indicators of potential risk.

---

## What Happened?

According to the DOJ, two individuals were charged in connection with operating a cryptocurrency money laundering service known as AudiA6.

Investigators allege the service processed more than $389 million in cryptocurrency transactions and advertised its ability to conceal the source of funds linked to criminal activity.

The investigation involved multiple international law enforcement agencies. Authorities conducted arrests, seized infrastructure, blocked related accounts, and dismantled websites associated with the operation.

What I found particularly interesting is that the service promoted anonymity, yet investigators were still able to reconstruct transaction flows through blockchain analysis and exchange records.

---

## Financial Crime Risks Identified

| Risk Area               | Why It Matters                                                                                                  |
| ----------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Money Laundering        | Mixing services may be used to obscure the origin of illicit funds.                                             |
| Cybercrime Proceeds     | Criminal proceeds from ransomware, fraud, and other offenses may move through mixers before reaching exchanges. |
| Wallet Obfuscation      | Transactions may be routed through multiple wallets to make tracing more difficult.                             |
| Rapid Movement of Funds | Funds are often moved quickly after receipt.                                                                    |
| Indirect Exposure       | Risk may exist even when no direct sanctions or criminal match is identified.                                   |
| Mule Account Activity   | Criminal networks may use multiple accounts to move or cash out funds.                                          |

---

## Red Flags Identified

Potential red flags in this scenario include:

* Deposits linked to known mixing services or wallet clusters associated with mixers
* Rapid movement of funds shortly after receipt
* Activity that lacks a clear business purpose
* Multiple transfers through intermediary wallets
* Customers receiving and quickly forwarding funds
* Transaction patterns inconsistent with the customer profile
* Exposure to ransomware-related or cybercrime-linked wallets
* Frequent movement between cryptoassets without a clear economic reason
* Concentrated activity involving higher-risk counterparties

---

## Investigation Questions

If I were reviewing this activity, I would consider the following questions:

1. Is the customer expected to engage in cryptoasset activity?
2. Does the customer's activity align with the expected profile?
3. Are there links to known mixing-service wallet clusters?
4. How quickly are funds moving after receipt?
5. Is there a reasonable explanation for the transaction pattern?
6. Are similar behaviors appearing across multiple accounts?
7. Is there evidence of mule-account activity?
8. Are counterparties connected to known cybercrime typologies?
9. Does the activity indicate attempts to obscure ownership or source of funds?
10. Does the activity warrant escalation for additional review?

---

## AML Analyst Perspective

From my perspective, the most important lesson from this case is that reviewing a broader pattern of activity often provides more insight than reviewing individual transactions in isolation.

A single transaction may not appear unusual on its own. However, when transaction velocity, wallet exposure, counterparty relationships, and customer behavior are reviewed together, a different risk picture may emerge.

Coming from a banking operations background, this reminded me of situations where reviewing a single transaction or exception was not enough. Understanding the broader context often provided a clearer view of the underlying risk.
